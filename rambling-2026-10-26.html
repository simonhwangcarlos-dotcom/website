I love rondos, or, I hate minimalism

October 26, 2026

Argh, I am behind. Believe it or not, I actually drafted two entries last week. But they do not make sense without audio, so while I figure that out, I will arrogantly ramble about something else. Haha, I have about as much nuance as a sledgehammer. But maybe it is more entertaining, to listen to someone who is arrogantly opinionated, than to listen to me hedging. When I was little, my grandfather once joked I only liked the smartest and the stupidest movements. I only wanted to listen to movements with a fugato, or skipped ahead to any movement marked, “theme and variations,” or “rondo.” But if you think about it, it makes sense. These are all forms where a small shape projects into the global architecture, and I always liked this very much, even in a building or a comic book. You would think, given this pattern, I would be a big fan of minimalism. But I am not. It is actually, the only style that I can confidently say I despise. The reason is actually because, I think the compositional principle is intellectually very successful. I just despise that principle. There are many branches to minimalism, but they mostly root back towards, a manipulation of time as a process. Often to create a trance-like, meditative response in the listener. I find most minimalist composers are very successful at doing this. Because, initiating a repetitive sequence will automatically focus perception to an isolated point. This is also, tangentially, the general principle behind work songs, the drummer in a historical army regiment, the music that plays when you call the insurance company, etc. It is a harnessing of perception to ease reality into something that slips by without notice. This, to me, is a baffling use of art. Art is always an illusion, almost, a propagandist reshaping of perception. It can be used to convince the perceiver of the intrinsic worthwhileness of enduring in perception, despite the suffering perception brings. The beauty of laboring to understand a world that is always slightly beyond grasp. Showing me, “Look, the human brain will create experience even out of a single tone! We are all limited by our consciousness," to me, that is pointless. Yes, of course that is true. What possible good does it do, to blot out the complexity of reality? If you are spending 12 hours a day mining, or marching through a field, or even waiting for the operator on the phone, that is one thing. But to me, it is almost repulsively self-indulgent, to wipe out conscious processing when you don’t need to. To me, it is a lazy desire to make the world easy. It is also, to me, aesthetically dishonest. A common analysis for minimalism is that it simulates a sort of perceptual enlightenment; a focusing on a slow, banal process, teaching appreciation for slow change and personal insignificance. But, to me, it has an almost opposite formal effect. If I spend four minutes listening to how Pärt slowly completes a scale, there is an underlying implication that my attention, my perception, is what makes an event meaningful. This is, almost unbearably self-important to me. The scale is an event, whether I am paying attention to it or not. The “event” of Spiegel im Spiegel is essentially, all of us paying attention to a scale. Human attention is what makes the piece worthwhile. A common counterargument, is that Pärt is uplifting an event we usually ignore. But that somewhat makes the piece, less about the piece than about me admiring my own ability to pay attention to it. I suppose, this could be my background as a performer superseding my ability to hold a philosophical argument, we regularly spend three hours attempting to perfect a random scale in a Mozart sonata. My time investment does not make Mozart’s scale important, the scale has a job to do in the form, and my job is to help it as much as I can. I suppose, a performer’s logic is not the target of minimalism. But still, if I am truly just a small node in the universe, then the intellectually honest thing to do is to open the door, and be blinded by the scale and motion of particulate. To reduce my perception to a single tone seems to be burying my head in the sand. It is almost, an attempt to argue, “My ability to hear this one note, rivals the scale of everything!” I think, this makes some people feel consoled by their importance. But I find it despicably self-centered. Of course, I am somewhat biased by my own perception. I can only see one small object at a time, and already can easily get caught repeating, “oxidative,” to myself for half an hour and not be able to stop. I hate meditation because it is just courting my already unbalanced wiring. So, quite arguably, I am not the target audience. Agh, as usual this was originally a thought about something completely different, in this case, why I think the Hammerklavier fugue is one of the most beautiful testaments of existence. So, very quickly, what I was trying to get to, was actually what I consider the inverse of minimalism. When a banal shape is used to create a form of brilliant intensity. There is a very beautiful comic book by Fumi Yoshinaga called, Antique Bakery. On the surface, the premise seems incredibly banal, “four handsome men start a bakery.” Yoshinaga, who has a hobby in fine dining, even joked that she pitched the comic because she wanted an excuse to draw pastries. But it becomes one of the most beautiful short form manga in the canon. What starts out as an absurdist comedy, slowly morphs into a story about loss, loneliness, figuring out how to live a meaningful life after something unbearable has happened. All told through a succession of normal business days at the bakery, jokingly fanservicing shots of tall men posing in suits, the chef training his apprentice, a clumsy waiter, and the owner unsuccessfully flirting with women who come to the shop. It is a brilliant argument for, how someone who understands form can create the most beautiful, uplifting object out of seemingly absurd material. So, the form does not need to be Hammeklavier level worked out, for me to find it beautiful. I actually think, certain applications of minimalism can be wonderful. It has a heavy influence on modern movie soundtracks, Philip Glass composed the score to The Hours, which arguably could only be orchestrated by a minimalist aesthetic. I think the final minute of Inception is an astonishing use of soundtrack. I find the minor mode fugues from Shostakovich Op. 87 and the third book of Ligeti etudes very beautiful, even though they use a similar sonority and process based philosophy, and I find many Reich pieces very exciting, especially to see percussionists do live. And, I bashed Spiegel im Spiegel earlier, but I find Pärt’s Cantus for Benjamin Britten and parts of Tabula Rasa very beautiful. Haha, if the scale is actually simulating motion, I find it quite wonderful. This is actually, a useful propaganda of the universe, to hear the scale racing by on multiple levels at once. To me, it is not a sin to be simple, it is a sin to claim that reality is defined by my perception, and to be purposefully blind to anything else. So, next week, if I cannot figure out how to insert audio clips, I will ramble a bit about Hammerklavier haha. But you can maybe see already why I find it so meaningful, by my tirade on the inverse philosophical principle.
