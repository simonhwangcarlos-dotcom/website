Glenn Gould

October 5, 2026

I forgot today was Monday. So, I cheat with a lazy thought for this week, just a recursive loop that I become preoccupied with every year around this time. Glenn Gould’s birthday was a little over a week ago. I am a big fan, but Gould is a bit of a cautionary tale for me. I must be very careful here, I am often unintentionally inflammatory through careless use of language. In a real, objective sense, Gould was outrageously successful. Shortly before his death, friends say he was looking into conducting, because he had already recorded all the repertoire that interested him. What other pianist can say this? That you have so exhaustively studied and interpreted all the pieces you ever wanted to learn in your life, that you have to move on to other instruments? I am almost certain, if Gould was informed of his entire biography, he would insist that he regarded his own life as a complete success. And who am I to judge? Everyone must make their own living and meaning somewhere. But, I suppose, this is a bit of the problem. Every year, around the end of September, all the classical music media profiles put up pictures of Gould with inspirational quotes. It is hard for me not to be contemptuous. I suppose, for a normal brain, the sentiment, “think outside the box, always be unapologetically yourself,” is useful. But Gould was using these statements to justify his extreme lifestyle. To a brain like mine, and like his, it is far more difficult to think like other people, than to think like oneself. Gould was, in a sense, absolving himself of confronting the most difficult cognitive task for his system. There is an interview where Gould is talking about his favorite piano. He says, completely convinced, listen to this piano in my recording of Brahms. It sounds like a completely different instrument! But it sounds like exactly the same strange piano, just now with Gould playing hammerblow style. I am sure it sounded quite different to Gould. But to anybody coming from the outside world, it is unmistakably the same, very odd sounding piano. This is the type of trap my brain naturally falls into. It is stagnation, disguised by outrageous amounts of energetic labour. Gould absolutely grew and improved as a pianist over his lifetime. But, in a sense, he appeared fully fledged, at a recording studio when he was 20, and stagnated there for the next 30 years. It is the same sentence repeated over and over, just with increasing focus on phonemes that were already effective the first time. If there is a common critique for Gould, it is mainly this lack of context in his interpretive machinery. Part of how he was able to output such a huge volume of work, was through a purposeful limitation on input. I do think, Gould was quite happy to stagnate. And, if you are that brilliant at 20, why ask for more? I am biased by my own paranoias I suppose. Because I also appeared into the world fully fledged, but was not brilliant. I never had the search for identity or growth in perspective that most humans describe. I am like a spider or wolf, I was born already knowing everything I’d ever be able to do. All my memories are recorded with the same stagnated equipment. I wonder often, if this is why I cannot speak or move properly. I did not have the extremely long plastic and fluid period that most humans have during the first 10-20 years of their life. I have always been exactly the same. I used to be just like Gould. I went wherever there was a piano I could practice on, and did not plan for much else. I learned very young, that nothing I did or said would change what happened to me, so I did not believe the world actually existed. If someone told me then, you will be mostly alone, you will spend every day trying to solve the same problems you’d already solved years ago, but you will have your own piano, no one will force you to eat things you don’t like or do things you hate. I would have taken that life in a heartbeat, and considered myself very lucky. But through a very strange set of confluences, my exoskeleton cracked in my late twenties. The ability to be changed by the world, to feel another mind outside my own. It is nauseating, to go back to the locked room of my brain after seeing what is outside. Gould suffered through various pains and technique nuisances throughout his career (I actually am fairly sure I know what was causing this, I used to sit very low as well, and suffered with exactly the physical symptoms he described. The short explanation is that, at a very low sitting position, the weight of extending the arm puts more strain on the shoulder girdle, and over time can compress the nerve leading down the neck into the fourth and fifth fingers, leading to numbness and weakness). He catalogued his symptoms obsessively, but when asked if he would consider changing his technique, Gould said, he worried he would be like the centipede. If he stopped to think about which leg to move first, he’d never be able to move again. I think Gould is correct. This is essentially what happened to me, I shed a moderately functional exoskeleton that kept the world out, and then had a huge slump at the end of my grad school, and a further reduction in ability. So, again, I don’t want to condemn his choices. If the world will obliterate you, it is much wiser to set a bounded enclosure. If opening the door will only leave you blinded and paralyzed to the onslaught of sensation, it is much more productive to keep the door closed. I don’t know if it will be possible for me to grow out of this molted crab state, I’m too old to expect much. But as impractical as it is, I wouldn’t redo the past ten years and keep my brain closed. It is a small solace, that even if I never accomplish anything with the rest of my life, I was able to change at least once in response to the world.
