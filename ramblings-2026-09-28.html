Explanation of Note Coloring System for Low-Vision Pianist

September 28, 2026

Watch video

Hello, I still do not have access to a piano yet, and I was too cowardly to go bang around on a public piano somewhere, to record a real analysis for this channel. But my speech is somewhat deteriorating, so I thought I would explain my note-coloring system. People find it interesting, and if I ever need to show score examples, you will see a lot of colors on the page First, I will explain my vision. I am legally blind, but I do have a small amount of vision. People ask sometimes, if with improvements in technology, my vision could be repaired someday. I mean, never say never, but I highly doubt it. The reason is because, the impairment is primarily in my optic nerves. I have a lot of conditions that affect my actual eyeball – my eyes shake and cannot hold steady; the eyeball is not round so it distorts light; I am partially color blind; my eyes don’t move together. But I never bothered to memorize the technical names. My eye doctor didn’t even feel the need to mention my color blindness until I specifically asked about it. The reason is because, I really don’t have enough vision that these conditions impact me very much. If you imagine a house built on quicksand, it doesn’t particularly matter if the windows won’t open and the electricity doesn’t work. Fixing those things won’t make the house livable. My primary condition is optic nerve hypoplasia. Like a lot of disabilities, this can manifest in a huge spectrum. Some people have it, and have almost normal vision. Some people have it, and have no vision. The optic nerves attaching my eyes to my brain are too small, and the wrong shape. I am closer to having nothing, but I still do have a small amount of vision. The easiest way I can explain it is, if you imagine a computer, and try to plug in a mouse with a shoddy wire. Some of the signal gets through, but not consistently and not all of it. So you can still sort of move the mouse around, it is just frustrating and tiring. No fix, like glasses or surgery, is going to change that the wire is not quite connected to the computer. This is very similar to my vision. Here, I will try to simulate it for you. Because my eyes shake, people ask if I get vertigo. I actually don’t, because my eyes just sort of turn off most of the time. They only turn on if something calls their attention, sort of like most humans with the sense of smell. So, I can see motion and colors fairly reliably. So maybe, my vision is actually more like this: Not a blurry image or a distorted image, just flashes of color and motion, but mostly nothing. Because my brain is lazy, it does not want to strain to see most of the time, so it just discards any visual information coming in through the faulty nerve. Unless I force it to pay attention, but this is tiring, and I can only focus on a tiny piece of the image at a time. This is a major problem for a classical pianist. I can technically see a score, but I cannot see the entire stave at the same time. Most notes just look like this to me. I can see if it is on a space or a line, but not much else. There is braille music, but it is very tiring and very intense to read. It is also, before braille readers, extremely cumbersome to carry around, and very difficult to get access to. And many pieces have not even been translated into Braille. So I never got good at it. Even most completely blind musicians that I know, don’t use it as their primary method for learning pieces. So, as a kid, I mostly cheated. I memorized by listening to recordings, and by laboriously figuring out what the first note of a passage was, and then just guessing by the direction the notes were going at any subsequent notes. If the line jumped more than a fourth, I would just leap wildly at whatever note seemed theoretically correct based on the harmony. And it was so painful to do this, I usually memorized the piece once, and then never looked at the music again. But this caused very weird distortions over time. There are a few recordings of me playing Tchaikovsky’s Seasons when I was young, where it is difficult to even tell what piece I am playing. So, eventually, I refused to play anything but Bach. Because, in Bach, it is mostly stepwise lines, it is easy to catch memorization mistakes, and a lot of the memorization is taken care of having good music theory. But this became a big problem when I was 16 or 17, because I had no repertoire to apply to schools with. And even now, most lush romantic techniques are a big problem for me. In high school, I met a mostly blind organist. He had synesthesia, so he programmed his computer to flash the color of the note he needed to play in time with the score. I thought this was interesting, but I have no computer skills. And, I have some motor difficulties due to Autism, I doubted, even if I could set this up, I would have the reaction time to be able to sightread. But then I went to a summer music festival. For anyone who has been to one of these, they are incredibly intense. You are essentially trying to fit a school semester into a 6-8 week program. You get repertoire very quick, and have to perform it within 1-2 weeks. And I had a strange but obvious realization. Most people spend most of their practice time as, “interpreting the score.” Then they remove the score at the end of the learning cycle, and do memory work separately. They are not struggling with both memory and interpretation at the same time. So I realized, I had to figure out a way to make it less painful for me to check the score. So I borrowed from the organist, in this slightly wonky way. Here is the Liszt b minor ballade, the system is easy to see here, because of the chromatic ostinato in the left hand. It is a note coloring system. Every pitch has a specific color. I use a tablet pencil, and trace over every note head with its specific color. For pianists, here is the color key: For music theorists, they might prefer a clockface, like this: The obvious disadvantage is, it is essentially a 12 tone system. So, it works as a pianist, because we have an enharmonic geography. Pitch class 1 is always this note, regardless of whether it is C# or Db. But it doesn’t work as well for strings, vocalists, etc. Because C# and Db are slightly different locations for them. My initial draft of the coloring system actually addressed this. It was a tonal system, where, instead of D is blue, A is red, etc; D was tonic, A was dominant, etc. etc. But this actually ended up not being helpful, for three reasons: Most pieces modulate. If you are modulating artfully, there is usually a swath of music that can be analyzed in multiple keys. The fact that you could be in two different keys is important. There was no way to color this using the tonal system. And, more importantly, my music theory was always very good. I didn’t need the score to tell me that A Major is the dominant. I needed a way for it to tell me, play A, C#, E. And, of course, not all pieces are tonal. So, I only demoed maybe a page of the tonal system before scrapping it. My second attempt, was to just translate normal notation to colors. So, all Ds would be blue, and then I would use accidentals and the key signature to tell me if it was D, D#, or Db. This would allow for the nuance of, a violinist’s D# is different than their Eb, so the color would be different. This was much better than the tonal system and much better than no colors. So I used it for about three months. But then, Messiaen broke my system haha. In theory class, we were given this piece: If you have a note moving from D to D# to Db to Cb to C, a name color system is worse than no colors at all. So, I reluctantly switched to my current system. 12 colors, each for a different pitch class. I was reluctant to make the switch, because, I can’t actually distinguish between 12 colors easily. For example, I hate reading scores in E major, because: I can’t really see the difference between the dark green I use for E, the dark maroon I use for G#, and the dark grey I use for B. I have to squint a lot when I am in this key. But overall, it has been incredibly helpful. My undergraduate teacher was really happy when I brought her the system for the first time. Because, she used to have to spend several lessons correcting all the places I had memorized the wrong notes. For the first time, I could bring her a new piece with mostly the correct memorization. And she could finally just say, start at measure 30, and I’d be able to do it. People sometimes ask if there is a reason for the colors. There sort of is. I don’t have synesthesia, but all the colors had to make sense for the note they represent. And then, I tried to finagle the colors so that they all somewhat relate to their fifth. Because notes a fifth away sound somewhat similar to one another. They don’t all work out, and the color relation rule is different in different keys, for example, D is blue and A is red, they are complementary. But C is grey blue and G is purple, they just look sort of similar. And some fifths, I just couldn’t get to work, like F# is beige and C# is this violent light blue. But I have used the system so much, that now I see those colors as being related, even though they technically aren’t haha. There are some disadvantages to the system. For one, it’s just very time-consuming. Coloring in one page of music takes me around 20-30 minutes. So before I can even start learning a piece, there is usually over 4 hours of note coloring work that I need to do. The other is just, user error haha. A pitch class system has no distinctions for octave placement. My teacher, he knew me very well, so everytime I brought him a new piece, he knew to check if I was playing everything in the correct octave. Occasionally I would just, color in a passage wrong. I still remember, I spent ages drilling this jump in Collines d’Anacapri, but colored in the G# minor triad as a B Major triad. My teacher caught it, but I had already put it maybe 20 hours of work drilling the wrong muscle memory. And even more exhaustively, it means I can’t easily compare different urtexts. I had a small panic attack over the phone with my duo partner once, because I had colored in the complete Debussy preludes in a pdf of the Henle edition. But then I went to the library and looked at the Durand. Debussy has such an unusual use of slurs. But Henle irons out his eccentricity into their standardized engraving system, which does make the score feel very different, and sort of indicates a different symbolic gesture. So I called my duo partner, because her mother is a professional pianist, and asked which publisher she used for Debussy. And finally decided, I had to go back and recolor the entire cycle, using the Durand engraving. This was equivalent to maybe 50 or 60 hours of work. But there are a lot of advantages as well. For example, this passage in Op. 110: Or, Berg Sonata: For most pianists, half the battle is just reading the correct notes. With the note coloring system, this isn’t an issue. The other big advantage, is a little obscure. And I can’t say it’s a definite thing, it’s just something I’ve noticed over time. Having to trace over the score in full, seems to build a sort of intuition for form, in a way that historically, musicians built by copying out scores. It used to be, if you wanted a score for your library, you had to borrow it and then copy it out by hand. Very labor intensive, you had to even draw out the staves. But it meant, you got a certain feel for how a composer uses the language. Similar maybe, to how writers sometimes copy out sentences they really like, to feel it in their body. Or artists using tracing paper to study a drawing. With the note coloring system, I don’t have to do the whole extraneous work of drawing out staves, but I do sort of trace over the composer’s hand. So as I am doing the coloring, I sometimes get a feeling, “Ah, this passage is odd. I’m not sure why.” And then discover later, that passage was filled in by a student, or has some transcription uncertainties, or just it is a formally odd place. Once, I got the opportunity to accompany a violist for BWV 1029. As we were rehearsing, I kept telling him, “This keyboard part is so strange, I’m not sure why, it just doesn’t feel like Bach’s keyboard writing.” Then, when we played it for his studio, the viola professor mentioned, that sonata is actually transcribed from a concerto, he had just performed the concerto version. I felt very pleased with myself haha. I have a lot of friends in computer science, they mentioned to me, it seems like a potentially programmable problem. If the right program existed, I could just upload a page of the score, and the program would color it in for me. But the problem is, I am the only market for such a program haha. Most low-vision or blind musicians figure out their own system or combination of systems. Also, the process of note-coloring has become an oddly beloved annoyance, like a dog who is always tearing up furniture. I do it when I am too tired or nervous to do any other work; I do it in the mornings while I drink coffee, or at night when I cannot sleep. I’d definitely choose to be able to see and read any score I wanted, if given the option. But I have become oddly fond of this system and its associated labor. So, that is my note coloring system.
