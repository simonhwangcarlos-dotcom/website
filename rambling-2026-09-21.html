Welt, ade: BWV 158

September 21, 2026

Another thought on translation, to buy myself another week to look for a piano. My speech is deteriorating though, so if I have not located anything to record on by next week, I will makeshift something together, in order to retain my speech. There is a devastating blow of form in this aria. I don’t want to spoil it, so please, do listen to this recording before reading any further: Listen to the recording Ok, now that Gardiner and Bach have revealed the secret, here are the two texts of this aria: Bass: Welt, ade, ich bin dein müde, Salems Hütten stehn mir an, Wo ich Gott in Ruh und Friede Ewig selig schauen kann. Da bleib ich, da hab ich Vergnügen zu wohnen, Da prang ich gezieret mit himmlischen Kronen. World, farewell, I am weary of you, The huts of Salem suit me better, Where I, in quiet and peace Can look on God in bliss forever. I will stay there, there I have pleasure in living There I will shine, adorned with heavenly crowns. Chorale: Welt, ade, ich bin dein müde, Ich will nach dem Himmel zu; Da wird sein der rechte Friede Und die ewig stolze Ruh. Welt, bei dir ist Krieg und Streit, Nichts denn lauter Eitelkeit; In dem Himmel allezeit Friede, Freud und Seligkeit. World, farewell, I am weary of you, I want to go to Heaven; There, will be true peace And the eternal noble peace World, with you is war and conflict Nothing but vanity. But in Heaven, at all times Is peace, joy, and bliss. The chorale line is so high, it is usually assigned to a soprano soloist. But Gardiner has a stroke of brilliance, and uses a children’s choir. The bass soloist sings, “World, I am weary of you, I want to return home,” and answering, in the almost uncannily pure voice of children, “World, I am weary of you, I want to go to Heaven.” An intrusion of angels into the stripped down, weary trio of bass, violin, continuo. It is a moment of such tenderness to shatter the heart. Bach has a reputation of academic, almost mathematical rigor. To the point where interpretation of symbology is almost discouraged, almost as if Bach’s score is a precursor to the hardcore serialism of Babbitt or Carter. But I find this a bit of a shame. Because at the center of Bach’s compositional muse is, religion, liturgy, the most flamboyant, narrative lens that humans overlay across perception. When I was in high school, I went through a difficult phase. But not in the rebellious, partying way that you would expect of a teenager. It is something perhaps only parents of autistic children can recognize; I stopped eating and mostly talking, I screamed for half an hour and banged my head on the wall and furniture, I got frustrated with my hair touching my ears and cut it all off with the kitchen scissors in the middle of the bathroom. I lost so much weight, with my scraggly bald head, people started asking my mother if I was sick. During this time, my grandmother convinced me to go stay with her and my grandfather, with the promise I could go hear his Tanglewood rehearsals. In the mornings, she would give me a simple task in the kitchen, and in the afternoons I would go with my grandfather to the half empty Ozawa hall. One morning, as we worked, she went through the Lord’s prayer with me, almost word by word. “Father” is not father, “heaven” is not heaven, “bread” is not bread; these words are merely symbols, attached towards a concept that is too huge to put into language. The feeling of receiving love and safety from something that understands the world infinitely better than oneself; the solace of a place where you can rest and belong; the worldly gift of substance, that God blesses the body with the ability to enjoy. When the cooking became too complicated for me to be of any help, she sent me to the living room with a copy of The Seven Storey Mountain. I could not believe in God, because to believe he had made someone as deformed as me, on purpose, would have made me resolve to go to hell. If I was to remain a good person, I had to believe I was just a bad roll of the dice. But I always remembered that morning with my grandmother. For years, I carried the printed out copy of the Lord’s Prayer she had given me in my pocket. Once, after fasting and refusing to speak for four days, I woke up with my heart making a painful fluttering motion. I snuck into the bathroom and filled the bathtub with the hottest water I could stand, and lay there repeating the Lord’s Prayer, and as much of the Rosary as I could remember, until the water got cold. I thought I was about to die. But I stood up, ate a quarter cup of rice from the leftovers of dinner, and went back to bed. This was meant to be a short thought on Bach, and it has ended up not to be. But I felt, I had to explain the symbolism of zealotry. There is an aspect, yes, of mathematical rigor to Bach. But it is a similar symbolism to the stark linguistic gestures of the Lord’s Prayer. The correct resolution out of an appoggiatura, the need for certain intervals to contract or expand, the weight of a doubled root or third; these rules do not exist because they have to exist, like the correct formula for light. They are containers, for the greater theology buried beneath everything. So, all this, is to explain my philosophy of Bach interpretation. The open arms of Christ are never so brilliantly three-dimensional than to a desperate man in the desert. The mirage of form. I am, at heart, always a technician, I love clarity in a pianist more than anything. But flamboyance in the service of form, is, to me, the pinnacle of Bach interpretation. Gardiner’s choice of a children’s choir is a wonderful example. The almost tangible border between perception and belief, at the horizon point where the land meets the sky.
