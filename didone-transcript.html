It is this, mad, inversional, certainty, but nihilism in certainty. So he creates this effect by having this resolute, rush down to the tonic, and this frantic, nihilistic rush up to the dominant. This Hegelian, antithesinal aspect of, can you hold one shape throughout the cognitive dissonance of the single shape implying directly oppositional concepts? And, Dido can’t. So: There, very important again. Chromatic scale! It’s another symmetrical object. It is also, the half step, a minor second, metastasized through the keyboard. It is also, a sort of wiping out of tonality, similar to the guillotine from Sufferings. It is also, a madrigalism, the flames eating through Dido’s body, the flames eating through the body of the automaton, eating through the form, and completely contaminating the motivic identities. Flames die down through all these fully diminished sevenths. And, Dido is in ash, the automaton is destroyed, the form, the motive is completely contaminated, the sonata is over. Conclusion So, zooming out, looking both backwards and forwards. Going back to Dussek, Suffering of the Queen of France. This is a narration in the third person. I, the pianist, I am acting almost like Virgil himself as an orator. I’m telling you “Marie Antionette, she died, but she went to heaven, she is safe and redeemed now.” But, Didone, Op. 50/3, it is an aria, it is a first person narration, it is, “Rise up from my bones, o great avenger.” But, Clementi, he’s a technician, and he’s a very endearing guy, he’s very mild-mannered, he has a technician’s steady temperment. He’s also a beloved teacher, his students are endearingly faithful to him. So, Clementi, he builds an automaton to contain this suffering for us. So Dido, she experiences this perceptual dissolution of the inversion, this nihilistic, suicidal rage. And then, she burns up, the automaton dissipates, and then, I, the pianist, and you, the audience, we can go, “Ah. Well, I’m glad that’s not me.” And shut the piano, and go live our peaceful lives. And Clementi himself, shortly after he publishes Op. 50, he will finish his magnum opus, the Gradus ad Parnassum, the teaching manual. And then he will retire, he will go live a very peaceful old age with his family in the countryside. He dies as a very old man, and it’s almost unremarked upon, except by his students. For Clementi, I think, in his ethos of the world, we all have a role to play. And unfortunately, for some of us, like for Dido, that role is to burn and to suffer. But I think, Clementi hopes, for himself, for his students, for us who play and listen to his music, I think he hopes we have a nice, quiet life. A quiet role in this mechanism of the world. So, he puts an intermediary, a cipher, like the actress on the stage, this form contains the suffering of perceptual dissolution. But, looking ahead a little bit. Beethoven, he was a huge Clementi fan, it’s actually very endearing. Because Beethoven loves functional form. He loves form with no excess fat. He loves the ethos of hard labor. And Clementi is an exemplar in all these things. But, Beethoven, he is not a gentleman. And, for some of us, it is our role, in the mechanism of the world, to live in this state of perceptual dissolution. So, Beethoven, he’s not going to give us the dignity of an intermediary cipher. He is going to force us to confront, how do you bear it, to live in this inversion of the symbol, this cognitive dissonance. But, for now, thank you for listening. Here is Clementi’s automoton, Op. 50/3, “Didone abbandonata, Scena Tragica.”
