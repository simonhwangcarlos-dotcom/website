An Arrogant Philosophy of Tempo (Part 1)

October 12, 2026

An arrogant philosophy of tempo (Part 1) Disclaimer: In school I was always getting in trouble for doing wonky things with the tempo. Most of my musical philosophy and sense of aesthetics is inherited from my grandfather. My philosophy of tempo comes from my grandfather’s philosophy of Mozart, from one of his coachings when I was little: Mozart should usually be played faster than people usually play him. But not too fast. You should play at the fastest speed you can get away with before anyone realizes you are playing fast. If you start to play, and the audience thinks, “Whoa, he is playing very fast,” that means you are either not thinking enough, or you are playing too fast. I liked this so much, that I thought about it in my offtime for years. Why is my grandfather correct? What is the general principle behind what he was explaining? This train of thought, concretized into my philosophy of tempo: Almost all pieces should be played at the speed at which musical objects pass by at the maximally comfortable rate for perception. This seems like a very obvious thing to say, until you look at standardized tempo choices in the Zeitgeist. Brahms is the most egregious example of this. I will leave aside late Brahms, there is the famous quote where he tells Clara the late miniatures should be played extremely slowly, so that complicates the analysis a bit. But if you look at something like, that last movement of Op. 10: Brahms marks the movement, “Andante con moto.” So, a moderate, walking pace. It is quite common to take the movement at quarter note = 60 bpm, or even slower. Ugh, unbearable. Haha, why am I being such a snob? Because, such a slow tempo interprets each of the falling eighth notes as the objects progressing “andante.” But the syntax is actually operating on a hypermetrical level. Every eighth note in the opening texture is not a singular musical object, but a participant in an arpeggio. Every bar represents one harmonic object. And the melody also, operates on the hypermetrical level, one to two notes per bar. Brahms’ syntax would be pedantically obvious if he wrote the opening in a compound meter, like 2 bars of 6/8 instead of 4 bars of ¾. But this would have looked insane on the page with Brahms’ harmonized figure, and implied something he wasn’t intending. So, Brahms’ andante should not be applied to every attack point, that is like using the attack points of the Alberti bass to determine the tempo of a Mozart sonata. The melody would be totally distorted and obscured. Brahms’ andante should be applied to the melody, which means, a hypermetrical andante. I am biased, but Katchen universally chooses good Brahms tempi: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=miRkuoSOwA4&list=RDmiRkuoSOwA4&start_radio=1 Quite often, I think Brahms is interpreted as being more difficult than he actually is, because people insist on such a slow tempo that the form is coming apart at the seams. So it takes a brutal intellect to hold it together. And, this can reveal some beautiful things, I will maybe discuss this next week, but just as preview, Pogorelich’s Op. 79/2, the slow turn of the monster’s head: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xXJCOqrqMis&list=RDxXJCOqrqMis&start_radio=1 But Pogorelich does this because he is a brilliant eccentric, and Op. 79 is so well known, he can afford to sacrifice legibility to reveal a hidden possibility of the form. If Pogorelich was premiering the piece, I would consider such a tempo choice as defamation. All the semioticians and linguists will be furious, but it helps also to compare tempo choice in music to tempo choice in speech. There are actually several types of speech that are unbearably boring to listen to: Very very slow speech. It is why a lot of baby programs are so difficult to listen to. The rate of linguistic information is so slow, it is hard to connect the objects to one another. This is like the standard tempo choice for Brahms Op. 10/4. Lack of thought, usually resulting in a fast tempo. When I started giving lecture recitals, my teacher forbade me from typing up a script and reading off it. At first I objected, because at academic conferences, this is what everyone does, and it makes it a lot easier to prepare. But then he pointed out, how many papers do you remember from a conference? The answer is, none. When you can read off a page, you can speak much faster than the audience can process, and the information just rushes in one ear and out the other. It is the opposite of the baby book problem, there are too many objects passing too quickly. This is like my grandfather’s warning, about how people should not notice you are playing fast. If they notice, it usually means information is passing by too quickly for them to comfortably catch it. A lot of surface information, very few objects. This actually covers a lot of different types of boring speech. The joke of the bad Shakespeare actor, who modulates on every single word; listening to someone try to tell you their dream; someone who keeps interrupting themself while telling a story, because they forgot to mention something; improv comedy (sorry, I know some people really love this, but I become terribly nauseous even if I find the jokes funny). These are all situations where there is a lot of surface information, either through vocal inflection, objects in the story, or literal words being said, but not many objects passing by underneath. So it is a combination of the first two problems, both objects passing by too quickly and too slowly. Pogorelich’s Op. 79/2 could arguably be put here, he makes the piece much more difficult to listen to than Brahms probably intended. But I forgive him, because he reveals something so marvelous in exchange for the endurance required of the listener. So, how does this relate to my grandfather’s coaching on Mozart? The Classical style is, purposefully, a style of immediately perceptible objects which pass by at a very enjoyable rate. And eclectic Mozart purposefully gives many interesting objects, it is part of what makes him so soothing, you never have to worry about your own intolerable inside brain distracting you from perception. Here is a beautiful lady. Before you get bored because she is just standing there, here is a soldier! Before his marching gets too predictable, here is a comical scholar playing a fugato. If you hang out on one object too long, the solace of Mozart is diminished, because the classical style does not maze into each object enough to hold your attention for long periods. Paradoxically, the solace of Mozart is partly created by perpetual direction. I have many thoughts on tempo, it is something I like to ramble about a lot. But I am already out of words, so I will leave with a recommendation. Rachmaninoff’s recordings of his own music are of course, incredibly useful. But I actually love him, as a pianist, more for his recordings of Mozart, Schubert, Handel, Chopin miniatures. The degree of control in voice leading is astonishing, especially given his giant hands. And his tempo choices are fantastic, across the board. He always chooses a speed at which it requires attention, but not exertion, for the listener to catch his objects.
