The Sufferings of the Queen of France

Annotated Bibliography

Quick note on structure: Hello, this is not a strict bibliography. It is just my recommendations, as a pianist (not an academic, theorist or musicologist), on sources I found particularly useful or entertaining. I have my own moral scruples about bibliographical conventions and their purposeful inaccessibility haha (admittedly, as a blind pianist, it is probably more inaccessible than it was intended to be). But I also want to give credit to the major sources that shaped my thinking. So I am using Chicago Name-Date style, as it is what most music grad students are trained to use. But an actual academic might find this list disturbing due to my colloquialism, my apologies. I have arranged the sources into a structure that is hopefully more useful for someone who is just looking to read more on various subjects mentioned in the lectures, rather than as an academic genealogy. Bibliographical sourcing is a little odd for this one. Dussek is one of those great composers who has been somewhat buried in time. He comes from the craftsman Classical age, where compositional output was overwhelmingly a necessity of livelihood, and only secondarily a matter of professional pride or identity. And unlike Clementi, who developed a systematic pedagogy and lineage of trained pianists, Dussek was largely transitory and only taught for income. So both Dussek and his close relations made very little effort to preserve records, letters, documents, etc. Even the biographical introductions for the urtext collection of his piano sonatas, published by Musica Antiqua Bohemia, is slightly confused on cataloging. Craw’s exhaustive catalogue (see below) reveals that Dussek often published multiple works/collections under the same opus number. For this lecture, this is quite important, because he published both Sufferings of the Queen of France and a piano sonata in Bb under “Op. 23.” So occasionally, even academically rigorous sources get the two works confused. English language sources have been quite difficult to locate and even more difficult to obtain. There appears to be slightly more written in Czech, as Dussek was born in Bohemia, now part of the Czech Republic. Here are the main sources directly on Dussek that I was able to actually locate a copy of: Carroll, Mora. 2003. “J. L. Dussek and His Role in the Development of the Piano Repertory.” PhD diss., The Open University. This is the one that I will imagine being the most enjoyable to pianists. Carroll relates Dussek, and his increasingly virtuosic writing, back to developments on pianoforte, developments in the ethos of artistic output, compares him to contemporaries (Clement, Hummel, Beethoven etc.) I actually had to cut most of Carroll’s research from this lecture in particular (Sufferings is actually not very idiomatically Dussekian, in terms of the piano writing), but I enjoyed it very much, and it was very useful in just achieving a somewhat stable sense of who Dussek was. Craw, Howard Allen. 1964. “A Biography and Thematic Catalog of the Works of J. L. Dussek (1760–1812).” PhD diss., University of Southern California. For anyone who has done any music academic writing, it’s worth it to take a look at this dissertation, just to feel the full blessing of the modern age of computers haha. Craw’s catalog is extremely impressive, and most of the information we do have on Dussek would probably have been lost without his enormous efforts in the 1960s and 70s to preserve what remained. Dussek is also cataloged using the system “Craw. (number),” this is from Craw’s work. The first half of the dissertation is a very restrained biographical overview, Craw uses almost entirely primary sources, the fragments of letters Dussek wrote to friends and publishers that still exist. The back half is a catalog of every published work, with the first few measures of each piece drawn out by hand, so that readers can check they have the correct score (this is important for Dussek, since several of his opus numbers have multiple publications attached). Thank god for computers and scanners haha. But my greatest gratitude to Craw’s exhaustive efforts here. Illiano, Roberto, and Rohan H. Stewart-MacDonald, eds. 2012. Jan Ladislav Dussek (1760–1812): A Bohemian Composer “en voyage” through Europe. Bologna: Ut Orpheus Edizioni. This was, for me, by far the hardest Dussek source to get a hold of, even though it is the most recent major publication on Dussek. If you are interested in Dussek and have either scholarly library access or a ton of extra money, it is worth it to look for a copy though. It’s much more readable than the dissertations (Caroll is writing primarily for other pianists and musicologists; Craw is extremely dutiful to historical ethics, but this makes the reading quite dry), and also serves as a sort of map pointing towards the few, difficult to locate sources on Dussek in English. Stewart-MacDonald, Rohan H. 2006. New Perspectives on the Keyboard Sonatas of Muzio Clementi. Bologna: Ut Orpheus Edizioni. Stewart-MacDonald is a huge source for the next lecture, on Clementi. This book is largely on Clementi, but it is built off of Stewart-MacDonald’s dissertation, which was more explicitly written on comparisons between the piano sonatas of Clementi, Dussek, and Beethoven, so he retains a lot of references to Dussek’s writing throughout the book. He was an English musicologist and fortepianist who passed away around 10 years ago. His research has been so extraordinarily helpful to me, and is becoming more and more difficult to locate. So I am trying to leave his name in as many places as possible, in an attempt to preserve it. Taruskin, Richard. 2009. Music in the Nineteenth Century. Vol. 3 of The Oxford History of Western Music. New York: Oxford University Press. Sufferings of the Queen of France is not a well-known piece, but a lot of musicologists are familiar with it, because of the ubiquity of Taruskin’s Oxford History in the classroom. In Vol. 3, Chapter 2, Taruskin uses Dussek’s guillotine as an example, he is demonstrating principles of, what we consider Romantic picturesque gesture, from before 1830. The funny thing is, I agree with his argument, I am just offended by the way he made it haha. Taruskin is a very good example of efficient academic writing, which I distrust (see Agawu, as counterexample, below) for its willingness to cherrypick evidence. My introduction to this piece was actually, when my teacher performed it several years ago, but, this passage from Taruskin was the first place I saw it mentioned in text anywhere, assigned for a music history seminar. On topoi/topical theory, the big one is of course: Ratner, Leonard G. 1980. Classic Music: Expression, Form, and Style. New York: Schirmer Books. It’s quite interesting, Ratner was writing in a time in which the “Composer Pantheon of Gods” ethos was much more accepted in academia haha. So he explicitly focuses on Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven in a way that would probably be considered sketchily divorced from context, for modern scholarship. But his paradigm of topical theory is so massively influential, that you will run into him very quickly, in any research on the Classical period. And it’s clear Ratner had complete fluency with the Classical world that produced Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven, the book is exhaustively researched, and Ratner constantly quotes primary source pedagogical documents and compositional discourse. So his focus on only Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, is more a matter of Ratner’s priorities than his fluency. The chapter which generated the sub-branch of music theory that is “topical theory” is at the beginning, but the entire book is quite interesting and readable. Ratner goes on to define various formal conventions, stylistic and regional idioms, changes across his three composers’ lives and between genres. Monelle, Raymond. 2000. The Sense of Music: Semiotic Essays. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. A more modern scholarly approach to topoi. It’s important to remember, Ratner’s original concept of topoi was somewhat more abstract than the way we tend to use it today. His usage is perhaps closer to Northrop Frye’s narrative genres; the paradigms are abstract models, not originally intended to serve as categorical boxes that we sort a piece into. So Monelle examines topoi from a more practical standpoint – what counts as a certain topoi, how do you actually locate a topoi, etc. In particular, he has a fascinating chapter comparing topoi to the leitmotif, which is what I was referring to mid-lecture. It’s beautifully written, perhaps not readable in a casual sense, but an example of very artistic scholarship. On Marie Antionette: Hardman, John. 2019. Marie-Antoinette: The Making of a French Queen. New Haven, CT: Yale University Press. I enjoy listening to history books, but honestly have a dreadful internal blueprint for any history that is not directly related to the piano repertoire haha. About two weeks before I needed to record this lecture, I woke up in a panic realizing I knew almost nothing about Marie Antionette, other than the obvious fact that she died. This is actually a huge can of worms, and for a few days I was overwhelmed trying to catch up. But then realized, as this is a lecture recital, and I am just a pianist, as long as I vaguely understand the big events, any minute biographical details would probably just bog the lecture down. So this is the only biography I finished reading. Honestly, I finished this one because, it was the most scholarly looking resource I could find that had an easily accessible audiobook. I enjoyed it, but can’t comment on the writing or anything, as I am not a historian (even musicologists and historians, famously, have quite a different approach to scholarship). And finally, these sources may not seem to be directly applicable, but they formed all the background cogs of my mind haha: Firstly, my favorite authors, Charles Rosen and Kofi Agawu. They shaped so much of my current thought, I have to cite them on anything I write. Agawu, V. Kofi. 1991. Playing with Signs: A Semiotic Interpretation of Classic Music. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. —. 2009. Music as Discourse: Semiotic Adventures in Romantic Music. New York: Oxford University Press. I am a huge Agawu fanboy haha. He can be tiring to read, but it is due to his extreme sense of academic ethics. Agawu often spends a few paragraphs clearly and concisely defining a concept, and then a few pages describing all the counterexamples, things that his definition should not be applied to, odd outliers that somewhat apply, etc. Some people find this too confusing, but once you have a sense of his rhetorical style and know to expect this structure, Agawu is a wonderful philosopher. He is beautifully and tenderly written, and very careful in his arguments. I have always been uneasy, about the widely accepted need to cut any contrary evidence when making an argument, so Agawu has always been, to me, a comfort and an exemplar of ethical writing. Playing With Signs is slightly dryer and more intensely focused on the Classical style and the interaction of topical theory with semiotics. Music as Discourse is much more wide-reaching, with chapters delving into almost every sub-branch of music theory (at least that directly applies to the core repertoire). If someone asked me to sum up my musical philosophy, I would probably just hand them a copy of Music as Discourse. I read it towards the end of my graduate program, for a seminar on core theoretical texts, and wondered, a bit amused, if instead of spending the last 5 years in school, I could have just read Agawu’s book. The place he arrives to, on linguistic analogs, Schenkerian theory, formal paradigms, motivic analysis, is basically where I ended up, after years of laboring back and forth between positions. But I think now, I did need to go through the process of arriving there myself, and probably would not have been able to digest the book without extensive background. So, just as forewarning, without some background in Schenkerian theory, linguistics, semiotics, Music as Discourse might be quite a slog, as you would be learning the theory at the same time as reading Agawu’s metacontextual discourse on how the philosophy of thought should be used. Rosen, Charles. 1988. Sonata Forms. Rev. ed. New York: W. W. Norton. —. 1997. The Classical Style: Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven. Expanded ed. New York: W. W. Norton. —. 1998. The Romantic Generation. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. —. 2002. Beethoven’s Piano Sonatas: A Short Companion. New Haven, CT: Yale University Press. Rosen is beloved for a very good reason haha. He is very much the pianist’s writer, in that he is very carefully, beautifully crafted in terms of linguistic form and prose, but also decisively practical. He is focused always on, what is this symbol, and how does one convey it? Which differs slightly from purely academic writing, which tends to focus much less on the actual action of projecting a symbol, in exchange for focus on recognizing a symbol. What separates Rosen from, for example, Brendel or Schiff, who also write beautifully, is his mastery of structure. Brendel tends to write the most beautiful sentences, the most useful descriptions, but bury them in the middle of a paragraph and immediately move on, almost like he doesn’t realize the magnificence of what he just wrote, haha. Rosen has the same artistic flair, but organized into a much clearer argumentative structure, if a sentence is important, it will be located in an important spot in the form. Rosen’s pianistic practicality makes him somewhat easier to read than Agawu, although the density of information is relatively similar. If Rosen says something, there is always a structural purpose for saying it, whereas large portions of Agawu’s writing is precautionary. Rosen being, primarily, a pianist, also allows him to also make judgements on compositional merit in a way that is very entertaining and memorable, and that he would not be able to make if he were a strict academic. So, for, particularly a pianist, but really anyone, wanting to get a clearer grasp on the major historical styles and classifications, I’d recommend starting with Rosen. Not because he is easy, but because most academic writing requires a huge volume of citational filler, which tends to make any argument much more difficult to extract from the form. The Classical Style is the big text that influenced this lecture in particular, but I included the other books because I was reading them all somewhat simultaneously, and even Beethoven’s Piano Sonatas goes into facets of interpretation of form and the historical context of Beethoven. Almén, Byron. 2003. “Narrative Archetypes: A Critique, Theory, and Method of Narrative Analysis.” Journal of Music Theory 47 (1): 1–39. —. 2008. A Theory of Musical Narrative. Bloomington: Indiana University Press. When I was TAing, one of my boss’ specialties was narrative analysis, so I would bug him about this when we had offtime, waiting for students etc. My mother is an English teacher and my father is a writer, so I had somewhat of a background in the analogous field for literature, and found it very interesting, so he assigned me the Almen when I took a seminar with him later. The theory of narrative genres, paradigmatic conflicts between order and disorder, etc., becomes more directly relevant in later lectures. But, particularly the reduction of thought to “disorder vs. order; who represents what; who is narrating?’ shapes so much of my analytical method, that I thought it was probably important to include in the bibliography. Cohn, Richard. 1997. “Neo-Riemannian Operations, Parsimonious Trichords, and Their ‘Tonnetz’ Representations.” Journal of Music Theory 41 (1): 1–66. —. 1999. “As Wonderful as Star Clusters: Instruments for Gazing at Tonality in Schubert.” 19th-Century Music 22 (3): 213–32. For the inversion from E Major to C Major. This is dense, beautiful writing, but very academic haha. “Neo-Riemannian Operations” is the big text for Neo-Riemannian theory, but Cohn is very much a theorist’s theorist, the final half of this article goes into almost mathematical, purely theoretical applications of the Tonnetz concept. Which is very interesting, but also tends to make grad students pull their hair out haha. “As Wonderful as Star Clusters” is much more artistically beautiful, and much much more readable. So if you want to do some reading in Neo-Riemannian theory, it is maybe easier to start here. Just be prepared, Cohn spends less time explaining the system, as he’s already done it extensively in “Neo-Riemannian Operations” and elsewhere. Which could be a good or bad thing, depending on preference, it means he can spend much more time applying the theory to actual pieces. I think if I remember correctly, the theory professor who recommended Cohn to me, as a piano performance student writing an essay on Schubert, had me start here, and only somewhat reluctantly let me ramble off into the denser Cohn and some Riemann and Hauptmann afterwards haha. Hatten, Robert S. 1994. Musical Meaning in Beethoven: Markedness, Correlation, and Interpretation. Bloomington: Indiana University Press. Zbikowski, Lawrence M. 2002. Conceptualizing Music: Cognitive Structure, Theory, and Analysis. New York: Oxford University Press. More recommendations from my old boss haha. These two texts seem to have very little to do with the lecture at hand, but they are, I think, very useful for developing an intuition on formal relevance. I realized, too late to fix it, throughout the lectures, I keep using the term “marked.” I picked up the usage from Hatten, but it comes from linguistics originally. It does still sort of make sense, with the colloquial semantics of the word, but it also has a very specific theoretical usage that is even more useful. Within a conceptual category, there is a broad spectrum of unmarked objects, and a much smaller sub-population of marked objects. Unmarked objects are definitionally assumed, but also provide less identity characteristics. Hatten gives the example of a population of “Classical sonatas.” In that conceptual idiom, “the major mode” is unmarked – major mode sonatas are the assumed paradigmatic baseline; but they also tell you very little about the sonata itself, the major mode can be used to signify many vastly different things. The minor mode is marked – there are far fewer minor mode Classical sonatas, and seeing one spikes attention. But the minor mode has a very restricted set of associations within the idiom. So, the marked subpopulation “minor mode Classical sonatas;” this label is less common, but gives you far more assumed information than “major mode Classical sonatas.” The Zbikowski is even more interdisciplinary, and delves so far into psychology, philosophy, and cognition that I feel apprehensive to attempt a summary haha. But, in my crude, pianist’s understanding, it is broadly about the cognitive state one rung below Hatten’s. How do we form the assumptions, the conceptual averages, that allow for the classifications of forms? So, very useful, just to have in the background of mind when doing form analysis – what is the conceptual average of this form, and how does this piece differ from that blueprint? The odd portions that stick out from the blueprint without conventional justification, tend to be identity features for the piece in question.
