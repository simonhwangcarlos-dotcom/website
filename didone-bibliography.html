Didone abbandonata

Annotated Bibliography

Quick note on structure: Hello, this is not a strict bibliography. These are just my recommendations, as a pianist (not an academic, theorist or musicologist), on sources I found particularly useful or entertaining. I have my own moral scruples about bibliographical conventions and their purposeful inaccessibility haha (admittedly, as a blind pianist, it is probably more inaccessible than it was intended to be). But I also want to give credit to the major sources that shaped my thinking. So I am using Chicago Author-Date style, as it is what most music grad students are trained to use. But an actual academic might find this list disturbing due to my colloquialism, my apologies. I have arranged the sources into a structure that is hopefully more useful for someone who is just looking to read more on various subjects mentioned in the lectures, rather than as an academic genealogy. Researching Clementi is a slightly different problem than Dussek. Like Dussek, there is not as much of a core shared scholarship around Clementi, as there is for Beethoven, Mozart, Haydn, etc. But Clementi is inextricably interwoven into piano pedagogy, and, compositionally, a huge stream of thought connecting from Bach, through the Galant style of the mid-1700s, through bel canto, singing styles in Romanticism, and finally into Brahms and even Schoenberg. And, Clementi, the technician, left a huge volume of primary sources, in correspondance, travel records, business deals, teaching practices etc. Only some of these are interesting haha, or, rather, they only become interesting only after absorbing such a huge volume of them, that they begin to form a cohesive character. So there is considerably more scholarship to read for Clementi than for Dussek, but a lot of it is more difficult for me to pinpoint as, “this source taught me exactly this.” I will begin again with the most directly relevant sources. And the biggest one, that I really want to highlight here, is the work of Rohan Stewart-MacDonald: Stewart-MacDonald, Rohan H. 2003. “Canonic Passages in the Later Piano Sonatas of Muzio Clementi: Their Structural and Expressive Roles.” Ad Parnassum: A Journal of Eighteenth- and Nineteenth-Century Instrumental Music 1 (1): 51–107. —. 2006. New Perspectives on the Keyboard Sonatas of Muzio Clementi. Bologna: Ut Orpheus Edizioni. —. 2008. “The Minor Mode as Archaic Signifier in the Solo Keyboard Works of Domenico Scarlatti and Muzio Clementi.” In Domenico Scarlatti Adventures: Essays to Commemorate the 250th Anniversary of His Death, edited by Massimiliano Sala and W. Dean Sutcliffe, 401–44. Bologna: Ut Orpheus Edizioni. So many of the questions I had around Clementi – the Scarlatti-like canons; the tripartite forms; the oddly static usage of harmonic syntax – Stewart-MacDonald directly addresses, in his book and (for my particular questions), these two articles. I found a scan of the chapter headings to his book, about six months into banging my head against Op. 50/3 due to Clementi’s odd, singular use of conventions, and was very excited. But then, almost immediately, found Stewart-MacDonald’s obituary. He passed away quite young, in 2017. It’s an unfortunate time in scholarship, it means most of his work is out of print and has not been digitized. “Canonic Passages” I had to write to the editor of Ad Parnassum, and they very kindly sent me a scan. Stewart-MacDonald did not include his analysis of canons in his book, so that research is almost completely lost. Stewart-MacDonald’s work, connecting Clementi backwards into Scarlatti and the Italian keyboard tradition, and forwards towards usage of the tripartite exposition in Romanticism, was such a keystone in me even understanding how to play Clementi. My teacher can attest haha, week after week for months after I’d technically memorized the sonata, I’d bring it in, and it just sounded very odd. Because I couldn’t understand what Clementi was trying to do, and I am extremely bad at faking certainty, I can’t even remember details well if I don’t understand why they are there. I owe Stewart-MacDonald a huge debt, so I am trying to leave his name in as many places as possible, in hopes his research will be preserved. And some more Clementi research, that I mostly used for constructing a biographical image of him as a person: Plantinga, Leon. 1977. Clementi: His Life and Music. London: Oxford University Press. This is the main English language biography on Clementi that is still easily accessible. Plantinga is an old school, very ethical musicologist. I admire this a lot, but it also makes the writing amusingly dry. Plantinga is quite careful not to imply anything that he doesn’t know for absolute certain. But, there are multiple events in Clementi’s life that imply something extremely dramatic – for example, in his twenties, he toured the continent, fell in love with one of his students, was banished from the province by her father, and disappeared into a rural church, where the priest recounts Clementi sitting in a corner all day for months, weeping and studying. Haha, very amusing and dramatic, but Plantinga is very careful not to turn it into a story, he records mostly only, the priest’s journal entries, a letter Clementi wrote to his father, general comments from friends and family about not knowing where he went. So, very admirable and useful scholarship, just not the most entertaining read. Brownell, Andrew. 2010. “The English Piano in the Classical Period: Its Music, Performers, and Influences.” PhD diss., City University London. Rowland, David. 2011. “Clementi’s Music Business.” In The Music Trade in Georgian England, edited by Michael Kassler, 125–57. Farnham, UK: Ashgate. Sala, Luca Lévi, and Rohan H. Stewart-MacDonald, eds. 2019. Muzio Clementi and British Musical Culture: Sources, Performance Practice and Style. New York: Routledge. These three sources focus on more specific facets of Clementi’s life, that for me, were helpful in trying to construct a picture of him, but maybe less relevant to my actual work of playing the piece haha. Brownell has a chapter on Clementi’s pianoforte company and the characteristics of Clementi fortepianos; Rowland exhaustively details Clementi’s publishing business and its various travails (makes me glad I am not an accountant haha. But gives a clear picture of Clementi, that he was able to handle this sort of business on top of everything else he was doing). The collection edited by Sala and Stewart-MacDonald is mostly very short chapters, cataloging one specific feature of Clementi’s biography or style. Well-written, but in terms of research for poor students, I would say, get it if the library has it, but save your money for Stewart-MacDonald’s book or one of the biographies. A lot of Clementi research is out of print, so if the library does not have it, it is quite expensive. This collection, like a lot of this type of collected scholarship book, is most useful as a roadmap for seeing who is researching the thing you are looking for, and what large scale research has already been done. Ok, those are the sources that will make obvious sense haha. These ones will make less sense, but I will arrange them historically which will make the pattern slightly easier to see. Clementi’s style is largely inherited from the Galant generation. This practice also branches into the Romantic piano pedagogy, and eventually the motivic overgrowth of late Romanticism/early expressionism: Bach, Carl Philipp Emanuel. 1949. Essay on the True Art of Playing Keyboard Instruments. Translated and edited by William J. Mitchell. New York: W. W. Norton. Burstein, L. Poundie. 2020. Journeys through Galant Expositions. New York: Oxford University Press. Gjerdingen, Robert O. 2007. Music in the Galant Style. New York: Oxford University Press. C.P.E. Bach is actually considered a separate style than the Galant, called Empfindsamkeit. His brother, J. C. Bach, who shaped the London taste of keyboard playing around when Clementi was making his London debut, is often credited as the father of the Galant style. The Galant style is sparser, more consonant, more singing, generally thought to stem into the high Classical conventions of Haydn, Mozart etc. Empfindsamkeit is more flamboyantly dramatic, more rapid turns of form. But the two Bach sons inherit a similar generative compositional thought from their father, this motivic, phoneme like style that I am describing in the lecture. The Art of Playing Keyboard Instruments is almost, to a modern reader, more like a composition textbook than a performance manual. Because, at the time, keyboardists were expected to realize figured bass, improvise, add artistic flourishes to a fully written score, etc. C.P.E. Bach’s process of elaboration becomes an explicitly articulated compositional style over the next 200 years. A lot of Clementi’s motivic elaborations stem from the same principles as Bach’s pedagogical instructions. The Galant style is very tricky to concretely define, it seems everyone has a slightly different definition. Scarlatti tends to be agreed upon as the godfather of the style, and J.C. Bach is its most devoted and successful adherent, but other than that, it’s difficult to pinpoint when and what constitutes a transition into the Galant from the Baroque, and out from the Galant to the high Classical styles. The Burstein and Gjerdingan are maybe, closest thing to a central modern text that most scholars cite when discussing the style. I actually read the Burstein first, for a forms class, and it made the Gjerdingan considerably easier. Burstein takes principles from Gjerdingan and shows how the practice influences the high Classical style, so many of the examples are from Mozart, early Beethoven, Haydn, etc., and are more immediately recognizable. Gjerdingan is writing, almost as a translation of a compositional pedagogy for the modern student. His concept of schemata is very interesting, but because (apart from Scarlatti) most of the Galant practitioners are not commonly performed, it was, for me, a lot more slow going to parse through. And since I am not a composer and do not like improvising, I got frustrated haha. But definitely, if you want as concrete a picture of an inconcrete category as you can get, Gjerdingan is the place to go, for the Galant style. Kallberg, Jeffrey. 1988. “The Rhetoric of Genre: Chopin’s Nocturne in G Minor.” 19th-Century Music 11 (3): 238–61. I actually had a whole section on Chopin in the initial draft of this lecture, I might move into a different lecture eventually. Chopin and Clementi feel, in the hand for a pianist, startlingly similar. And it is, again, this phoneme, rhetoric principle; motivic writing used as speech, harmony used as punctuation. And in Chopin, the gesture becomes more explicitly vocal, the famous bel canto writing of the late Classical/early Romantic period. Solie, Ruth A. 1980. “The Living Work: Organicism and Musical Analysis.” 19th-Century Music 4 (2): 147–56. McClary, Susan. 1993. “Narrative Agendas in ‘Absolute’ Music: Identity and Difference in Brahms’s Third Symphony.” In Musicology and Difference: Gender and Sexuality in Music Scholarship, edited by Ruth A. Solie, 326–44. Berkeley: University of California Press. Schoenberg, Arnold. 1984. “Brahms the Progressive.” In Style and Idea: Selected Writings of Arnold Schoenberg, edited by Leonard Stein, translated by Leo Black, 398–441. Berkeley: University of California Press. And finally, connection to organicism. There is so much written on the topic, it is a little overwhelming. But the Solie is a very beautifully written, very precise definition of the concept and its history. The McClary, I do feel a little guilty to include here, McClary is actually making a much more serious argument than I was using her thought for in this lecture. She is arguing that, all music is somewhat contaminated by the prejudices of the age, even the idealistic absolute music – motives still have tinges of prejudice, an “oriental” theme; a “feminine” cadence – even if the piece is not outright mocking someone from the outside world. I will probably have to address this to some extent, when I get to Debussy. But I am not good at that sort of thing and am dreading it haha. For here, I was mostly using her thought of the motive as a concept that cannot be purely intramusical. The Schoenberg is one of those articles everyone has to read at least once in grad school haha. I also actually used to have a long passage on Schoenberg’s theory of the motive, motivic liquidation, motivic fragmentation, etc, but ended up having to cut it. I might try to put it elsewhere with the Chopin. What I was mostly using the essay for, is Schoenberg’s tracking of overlays upon thought, his philosophy of composition is just a logical extension of this long practice of motivic based composition. Brahms, who was famously a traditionalist, was always looking back at Baroque and Classical practices and conventions. Schoenberg argues, that Brahms’ overlay of thought onto these practices naturally implied his own further overlay. So I was using the same tracing gesture, for most of the motivic analysis in this lecture. And finally, these sources may not seem to be directly applicable, but they formed all the background cogs of my mind haha: Firstly, my favorite authors, Charles Rosen and Kofi Agawu. They shaped so much of my current thought, I have to cite them on anything I write. Agawu, V. Kofi. 1991. Playing with Signs: A Semiotic Interpretation of Classic Music. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. —. 2009. Music as Discourse: Semiotic Adventures in Romantic Music. New York: Oxford University Press. I am a huge Agawu fanboy haha. He can be tiring to read, but it is due to his extreme sense of academic ethics. Agawu often spends a few paragraphs clearly and concisely defining a concept, and then a few pages describing all the counterexamples, things that his definition should not be applied to, odd outliers that somewhat apply, etc. Some people find this too confusing, but once you have a sense of his rhetorical style and know to expect this structure, Agawu is a wonderful philosopher. He is beautifully and tenderly written, and very careful in his arguments. I have always been uneasy, about the widely accepted need to cut any contrary evidence when making an argument, so Agawu has always been, to me, a comfort and an exemplar of ethical writing. Playing With Signs is slightly dryer and more intensely focused on the Classical style and the interaction of topical theory with semiotics. Music as Discourse is much more wide-reaching, with chapters delving into almost every sub-branch of music theory (at least that directly applies to the core repertoire). If someone asked me to sum up my musical philosophy, I would probably just hand them a copy of Music as Discourse. I read it towards the end of my graduate program, for a seminar on core theoretical texts, and wondered, a bit amused, if instead of spending the last 5 years in school, I could have just read Agawu’s book. The place he arrives to, on linguistic analogs, Schenkerian theory, formal paradigms, motivic analysis, is basically where I ended up, after years of laboring back and forth between positions. But I think now, I did need to go through the process of arriving there myself, and probably would not have been able to digest the book without extensive background. So, just as forewarning, without some background in Schenkerian theory, linguistics, semiotics, Music as Discourse might be quite a slog, as you would be learning the theory at the same time as reading Agawu’s metacontextual discourse on how the philosophy of thought should be used. Rosen, Charles. 1988. Sonata Forms. Rev. ed. New York: W. W. Norton. —. 1997. The Classical Style: Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven. Expanded ed. New York: W. W. Norton. —. 1998. The Romantic Generation. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. —. 2002. Beethoven’s Piano Sonatas: A Short Companion. New Haven, CT: Yale University Press. Rosen is beloved for a very good reason haha. He is very much the pianist’s writer, in that he is very carefully, beautifully crafted in terms of linguistic form and prose, but also decisively practical. He is focused always on, what is this symbol, and how does one convey it? Which differs slightly from purely academic writing, which tends to focus much less on the actual action of projecting a symbol, in exchange for focus on recognizing a symbol. What separates Rosen from, for example, Brendel or Schiff, who also write beautifully, is his mastery of structure. Brendel tends to write the most beautiful sentences, the most useful descriptions, but bury them in the middle of a paragraph and immediately move on, almost like he doesn’t realize the magnificence of what he just wrote, haha. Rosen has the same artistic flair, but organized into a much clearer argumentative structure, if a sentence is important, it will be located in an important spot in the form. Rosen’s pianistic practicality makes him somewhat easier to read than Agawu, although the density of information is relatively similar. If Rosen says something, there is always a structural purpose for saying it, whereas large portions of Agawu’s writing is precautionary. Rosen being, primarily, a pianist, also allows him to also make judgements on compositional merit in a way that is very entertaining and memorable, and that he would not be able to make if he were a strict academic. So, for, particularly a pianist, but really anyone, wanting to get a clearer grasp on the major historical styles and classifications, I’d recommend starting with Rosen. Not because he is easy, but because most academic writing requires a huge volume of citational filler, which tends to make any argument much more difficult to extract from the form. The Classical Style is the big text that influenced this lecture in particular, but I included the other books because I was reading them all somewhat simultaneously, and even Beethoven’s Piano Sonatas goes into facets of interpretation of form and the historical context of Beethoven. Almén, Byron. 2003. “Narrative Archetypes: A Critique, Theory, and Method of Narrative Analysis.” Journal of Music Theory 47 (1): 1–39. —. 2008. A Theory of Musical Narrative. Bloomington: Indiana University Press. When I was TAing, one of my boss’ specialties was narrative analysis, so I would bug him about this when we had offtime, waiting for students etc. My mother is an English teacher and my father is a writer, so I had somewhat of a background in the analogous field for literature, and found it very interesting, so he assigned me the Almén when I took a seminar with him later. The theory of narrative genres, paradigmatic conflicts between order and disorder, etc., becomes more directly relevant in later lectures. But, particularly the reduction of thought to “disorder vs. order; who represents what; who is narrating?’ shapes so much of my analytical method, that I thought it was probably important to include in the bibliography. Hatten, Robert S. 1994. Musical Meaning in Beethoven: Markedness, Correlation, and Interpretation. Bloomington: Indiana University Press. Zbikowski, Lawrence M. 2002. Conceptualizing Music: Cognitive Structure, Theory, and Analysis. New York: Oxford University Press. More recommendations from my old boss haha. These two texts seem to have very little to do with the lecture at hand, but they are, I think, very useful for developing an intuition on formal relevance. I realized, too late to fix it, throughout the lectures, I keep using the term “marked.” I picked up the usage from Hatten, but it comes from linguistics originally. It does still sort of make sense, with the colloquial semantics of the word, but it also has a very specific theoretical usage that is even more useful. Within a conceptual category, there is a broad spectrum of unmarked objects, and a much smaller sub-population of marked objects. Unmarked objects are definitionally assumed, but also provide less identity characteristics. Hatten gives the example of a population of “Classical sonatas.” In that conceptual idiom, “the major mode” is unmarked – major mode sonatas are the assumed paradigmatic baseline; but they also tell you very little about the sonata itself, the major mode can be used to signify many vastly different things. The minor mode is marked – there are far fewer minor mode Classical sonatas, and seeing one spikes attention. But the minor mode has a very restricted set of associations within the idiom. So, the marked subpopulation “minor mode Classical sonatas;” this label is less common, but gives you far more assumed information than “major mode Classical sonatas.” The Zbikowski is even more interdisciplinary, and delves so far into psychology, philosophy, and cognition that I feel apprehensive to attempt a summary haha. But, in my crude, pianist’s understanding, it is broadly about the cognitive state one rung below Hatten’s. How do we form the assumptions, the conceptual averages, that allow for the classifications of forms? So, very useful, just to have in the background of mind when doing form analysis – what is the conceptual average of this form, and how does this piece differ from that blueprint? The odd portions that stick out from the blueprint without conventional justification, tend to be identity features for the piece in question.
