Auf, auf, mein Herz! - I am always a zealot, even as amateur translator

September 12, 2026

Auf, auf, mein Herz, und du mein ganzer Sinn; Wirf alles das, was Welt ist, von dir hin! Wo das du willt, was göttlich ist, erlangen, So lass den Leib, in dem du bist gefangen. Wake up, wake up! My heart, and you, my entire mind! All that which makes up the world, throw it away from you! That which is of God, if that is where you wish to attain, So leave behind the body, in there you are imprisoned. Translation notes: 1. “Auf, auf,” to “Wake up, wake up”: This is a wonderfully flamboyant opening gesture, some printings of the chorale text even write it separated from the rest of the line: “Auf, auf! Mein Herz, und du mein ganzer Sinn.” The German literally translates to something closer to, “up, up,” or “arise, arise.” But, these statements, in English, are not as rhetorically pedantic as “auf, auf.” So, I am translating more to, the broad symbol behind the opening object, than to the literal words. It is a rousing call. A linguistic gesture that is metaphorically similar to the bells of the church before mass. “Wake up, wake up,” is definitely much more specific than “auf, auf.” But if you conceive of the “action of arousing attention” as the metaphorical object, rather than the word itself, “auf,” the implication becomes similar, while retaining flamboyance. To emphasize the opening gesture as expository, I broke it off with the sometimes included exclamation point. 2. “Herz - Sinn - Leib,” to “heart - mind - body.”: These three objects are the essential conceptual locations orienting the function of the text. “Sinn” is not exactly, the English concept of “mind.” It is perhaps, an object in-between “mind,” “the sense,” “the consciousness,” “awareness.” But the tripartite contrast is so essential to the function of the form, that the neat English split into, heart - mind - body, is maybe worth the reduction. The speaker of the chorale seems to be primarily addressing Sinn. The bells of “Auf, auf” are used to awaken two entities, Herz, and Sinn. Herz (heart), tenderly, needs no further instruction. An implication that the heart already knows God. But Sinn, is specifically singled out, “und du, mein ganzer Sinn.” Perhaps, the mind suffers upon waking, the inherent doubt and pain of thought. So the speaker seems to address him in the 2nd-4th lines, and instructs him to throw away Leib (the body), the object imprisoning him, preventing him from knowing God. The three objects comprising the self create the contrast which, in turn, creates the form. The heart knows God already. The mind doubts and must be persuaded. The body is, “of the world,” and must be eventually discarded, if the mind is to reach God. It is almost painfully tender, it is one of my very favorite chorales. So, to make the rhetorical form as clear as possible, I have changed the punctuation a bit for the English. 3. “was Welt ist; was göttlich ist” to “that which makes up the world; that which is of God”: This was a struggle, English does not have an elegant syntax to convey what the German does. “Was Welt ist” is a categorical statement. Something close to “everything that falls within the category “world.”” The broad gesture is often translated as something approximating the common Christian sentiment, “throw away worldliness to achieve the divine.” But, this has an imported connotation that, I think, obscures the tenderness of the chorale. In English, “worldliness” often has a connotation of refinement, cleverness. And “divine” has a colloquial connotation of ecstatic, thoughtless bliss. This contrast introduces a tinge of an idea that, I think, is not implied by the German – that thought itself is one of the worldly objects which Sinn must discard; and that the world of God is one of thoughtless joy. There is a slightly condemnatory undertone to the commonly used English translation, something vaguely parallel to the crude reduction, “if you’d just stop thinking, you’d be happy all the time.” When, thinking, is fundamentally part of what Sinn is. The German is, I think, more tender, and wiser. The underlying theology does seem to indicate, a sacrifice of the individual self is necessary in order to reach God. But the chorale presents the sacrifice almost more as a change of location, an escape from suffering, the body imprisons Sinn, who longs to belong with God. So, to try to carry this across, I altered the sentence construction a bit. The German “was Welt ist,” is definitionally, not “that which makes up the world.” And “was göttlich ist” is not definitionally, “that which is of God.” But I wanted the articulation to emphasize that the thing which the form contrasts is, two conceptual domains. So this is also, why I moved both linguistic chunks to the beginning of their respective lines. 4. “Wo… so” to “if that is where… so”: This is, again, me trying to emphasize the state/location change implied by the German. “Wo… so” is a conditional clause, somewhere between “where/when/if… so.” There is not a direct English corollary to this positional/temporal conglomerate, so I used the slightly clunky, “if that is where… so”
